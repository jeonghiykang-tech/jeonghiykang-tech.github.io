USMCA / CUSMA rules of origin for steel and auto parts: a practical guide

By Michelle Liu, CCS, CTCS · Updated November 13, 2026

"Made in Canada" does not automatically mean "qualifies under USMCA." For steel processors and automotive parts suppliers, origin depends on the product-specific rule for the tariff classification — and on records that can survive a CBSA or CBP verification. This guide explains how it works in practice.

2026 status at a glance

  • At the first joint review on July 1, 2026, the United States did not confirm a 16-year extension. Canada and Mexico did.
  • The agreement remains in force. Preferential tariffs and rules of origin still apply.
  • The parties must now hold a joint review every year. If no extension is agreed, the agreement ends on July 1, 2036.
  • No changes to the rules of origin had been adopted as of this update.

USMCA, CUSMA and T-MEC are the same agreement

The United States calls it USMCA, Canada calls it CUSMA (Canada–United States–Mexico Agreement), and Mexico calls it T-MEC. It replaced NAFTA on July 1, 2020. The rules of origin are in Chapter 4 and its annexes, with additional automotive rules in the Appendix to Annex 4-B.

Three ways a good can qualify

  1. Wholly obtained or produced in the territory — rare for manufactured steel or auto parts.
  2. Produced exclusively from originating materials — every input already qualifies.
  3. Meets the product-specific rule of origin for its tariff classification. This is where most steel and auto parts decisions are made.

Product-specific rules: tariff shift and regional value content

Each HS heading or subheading has its own rule. Most rules use one or both of these tests:

  • Tariff shift — every non-originating material must change to a different chapter, heading or subheading during production in North America.
  • Regional value content (RVC) — a minimum percentage of the good's value must be North American. RVC is calculated using the transaction value method or the net cost method. Some automotive goods, including passenger vehicles and light trucks, must use the net cost method.

Because the rule depends on classification, a classification error can cause an origin error. Always confirm the HS code first.

Steel: why processing in Canada may not be enough

Flat-rolled steel (Chapter 72) is often sourced from one country and processed in another. Simple processing such as slitting, cutting to length or blanking often does not change the tariff classification. If the rule requires a tariff shift, the processed steel may not qualify even though the work happened in Canada. Steel from outside North America that is only cut or slit in Canada should be reviewed carefully before anyone signs a certification.

The country where steel was melted and poured is also increasingly important — for U.S. Section 232 duties and for the automotive steel purchase rule below. Keep mill certificates and melt/pour records with your origin file.

Automotive rules: stricter than the general rules

RequirementWhat it means
Vehicle RVCPassenger vehicles and light trucks need 75% regional value content (fully phased in July 1, 2023).
Core partsCore parts such as the engine, transmission, body and chassis, axle, suspension, steering and advanced battery must originate for the vehicle to qualify. Core, principal and complementary parts each have their own RVC thresholds.
Steel and aluminum purchasesVehicle producers must show that at least 70% of their steel and aluminum purchases (by value) originate in North America.
Steel melt-and-pourBeginning July 2027, steel must be melted and poured in North America to count toward the 70% steel requirement.
Labour value content40% of a passenger vehicle's value and 45% of a light truck's value must come from high-wage production (average base wage of at least US$16 per hour).

For a parts supplier, this means OEM customers will ask for more than a yes/no origin answer. Expect requests for RVC data, steel and aluminum origin, and melt/pour information for the steel in your parts.

Certifications of origin

  • USMCA has no mandatory form. A certification can be completed by the importer, exporter or producer if it contains the required minimum data elements.
  • A blanket certification can cover multiple shipments of identical goods for up to 12 months.
  • The certifier must have the documents that support the claim, and records must be kept for at least five years under the agreement (domestic rules may require longer).
  • If you learn that a certification is wrong, you must notify the people you gave it to and correct it.

Common mistakes I see at steel and auto parts companies

  • Treating "made in Canada" or "shipped from Canada" as proof of origin.
  • Relying on supplier certifications that expired or no longer match the bill of materials.
  • Not re-checking origin when a steel supplier, mill or coil source changes.
  • Classifying a part incorrectly, which applies the wrong rule of origin.
  • Not separating Canadian-origin, U.S.-origin and non-North American steel in inventory records.

Why origin matters even more under U.S. tariffs

USMCA qualification affects how some U.S. Section 232 tariffs apply. For example, USMCA-qualifying auto parts have been treated differently from non-qualifying parts under the Section 232 auto parts tariff. See my 2026 Section 232 guide for steel and auto parts.
